Does an implanted brain electrode actually need to be placed precisely?

The honest answer: "precise" is not an absolute virtue. It's a ratio — set by how local your signal is, and how far your electrode can actually hear.

A thread on the physics.

------------------------------------------------------------

Precision only becomes a question if two things hold: the signal you want lives in a specific place, and the electrode hears only a small volume around it.

If signal were smeared brain-wide, or one contact could hear all of it, placement wouldn't matter.

------------------------------------------------------------

So — is function localized or distributed?

Localization: Broca (human), Fritsch & Hitzig (dog). Holism: Lashley (rat), where the deficit tracked how much cortex you removed, not which part.

Reality sits between them, and exactly where it sits is the whole answer.

------------------------------------------------------------

Consensus, three layers:

1. Macro address is real — region + topographic maps
2. How local depends on signal type: state/movement goes brain-wide, percept & choice stay restricted (Steinmetz, Stringer; mice)
3. Within a region: distributed and redundant (Rigotti; macaque)

------------------------------------------------------------

Layers 1 and 3 squeeze out a standard.

Lower bound: you must land in the right region. Miss it and no decoder recovers it — the information isn't inside the listening radius.
Upper bound: you needn't hit a specific neuron.

So the scale is millimetres, not microns.

------------------------------------------------------------

Which makes "precise enough" an inequality:

how far you actually miss < how far you're allowed to miss

Tolerance shrinks with small targets, with dangerous neighbours (a vessel, another nucleus), and with finer signals. The miss itself grows with depth.

------------------------------------------------------------

Depth is pure geometry: 1° of angular error over 80 mm is 1.40 mm at the tip.

It shows up in real surgery. Across 500 sEEG procedures, 6496 electrodes: median entry error 1.43 mm, median target error 1.77 mm.

Same trajectory, worse the deeper it goes.

------------------------------------------------------------

What sets the tolerance is the listening radius. And an electrode never "hears a neuron" — it measures extracellular potential at one point, V = I/(4πσr), falling off with distance.

The radius is simply where that decay meets the noise floor.

------------------------------------------------------------

Measured, it's three zones rather than one line:

<50 µm — sortable single units
50–140 µm — above noise, but you can't tell which neuron
>140 µm — background only

Change frequency band and the scale jumps: LFP reaches 250–500 µm, into mm when neurons fire in sync.

------------------------------------------------------------

Two knobs, two different physics, constantly conflated.

Impedance → noise. Effective surface area sets impedance, impedance sets thermal noise. Lower is simply better.

Footprint → range. A wide contact spatially averages, which low-passes the spikes away.

------------------------------------------------------------

The trick: those are two different areas. A porous coating (PEDOT, Pt black, IrOx) raises microscopic surface area without widening the footprint.

Low impedance and single-unit resolution at once — which is why you don't just make electrodes as small as possible.

------------------------------------------------------------

Now flip to stimulation. Different physics.

You inject current, so the quantity is the volume of tissue activated. Threshold grows with the square of distance: 10 µA reaches ~85 µm (macaque motor cortex).

Recording's radius is fixed. Stimulation's you can dial.

------------------------------------------------------------

And the counterintuitive part: stimulation doesn't drive the somata beside the contact. Axons have lower thresholds — fibres of passage included.

Two-photon imaging in mouse cortex: microstimulation excites a sparse, scattered set, sometimes mm away, by exciting axons directly.

------------------------------------------------------------

So one electrode reaches two different populations: local somata when it listens, a distributed axonal set when it drives.

"Precise" was never absolute. It's relative to the signal you chose and the physics of your contact.
