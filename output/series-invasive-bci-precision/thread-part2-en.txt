Part 2 on precision in implanted BCIs.

Part 1 ended on a static picture: how far an electrode reaches, in space. Add time and three separate mechanisms erode it — and the standard fix, "just add more electrodes," turns out to have a boundary.

------------------------------------------------------------

Three mechanisms, each with its own timescale:

1. Mechanical — micromotion at the interface
2. Biological — encapsulation by a foreign body response
3. Representational drift — the neural code changes

First two break the container. The third moves the target.

------------------------------------------------------------

Micromotion, measured in humans: phase-contrast MRI puts peak brain displacement relative to the skull at 0.1–0.5 mm per cardiac cycle.

Compare that with the single-unit zone from Part 1, ~50 µm. The brain shifts several times that distance every heartbeat.

------------------------------------------------------------

Why it matters is stiffness. Silicon ~165 GPa, brain tissue ~1–10 kPa — seven to eight orders of magnitude apart.

A rigid probe barely deforms, so tissue deforms around it and strain concentrates at the interface. At ~1 Hz that's ~10⁵ cycles a day.

------------------------------------------------------------

That constant micro-injury keeps a wound response from ever resolving. Astrocytes peak in week 1 and form a dense sheath by weeks 2–3.

The chronic phase is what isolates the electrode — not the insertion injury itself.

------------------------------------------------------------

And it attacks Part 1's listening radius from both ends.

Space: neuron density falls ~40% within 100 µm; by 4–8 weeks the nearest healthy neuron can sit hundreds of µm out.
Noise: encapsulation raises impedance, which raises the noise floor.

------------------------------------------------------------

Layer 3 is different — the container can be perfectly fine.

Representational drift: electrode stable, same neurons, SNR unchanged, same behaviour, yet how those neurons encode task variables slowly changes.

Not a failure. A property of the code.

------------------------------------------------------------

How fast depends heavily on region.

Hippocampus (mouse): in one familiar environment, only ~15–25% of place cells overlap between any two days.

Motor cortex (monkey, 2 years): single units turn over and drift, but the low-dimensional manifold holds its shape.

------------------------------------------------------------

That split has a practical edge. A fixed decoder built on raw activity degrades; one built on the stable manifold decoded reliably across two years.

Note the asymmetry too: drift is recoverable by recalibration. Encapsulation is physical — no algorithm undoes it.

------------------------------------------------------------

Three unrelated problems, one shared rescue — information is spread across many neurons, not locked into any one:

Bad placement → scatter electrodes, some land well
Channels die → enough good ones remain
Drift → units move, the manifold doesn't

------------------------------------------------------------

The drift fix is elegant. What changes is the coordinate frame, not the shape.

Reduce a day's population activity to 2D and reach directions trace a ring. Weeks later the ring is intact but rotated, so a position-based decoder misreads it.

------------------------------------------------------------

So rotate it back — and you can recover the angle from the temporal structure of the dynamics alone, without knowing what the user intended.

That's the unsupervised part. It works because the dynamics are rich enough to pin the rotation uniquely.

------------------------------------------------------------

But redundancy isn't free. It needs information spread across many neurons AND low-dimensional relative to your sampling.

Motor cortex qualifies: the behaviourally relevant subspace runs ~10 dimensions (7, 13 and 9 across three monkeys).

------------------------------------------------------------

Sparse codes break it. Concept cells in human MTL: one specific concept fires very few, highly selective neurons. And in mouse, choice and stimulus signals are both sparse and spatially restricted.

Scatter electrodes at random and you miss them.

------------------------------------------------------------

Which flips Part 1's conclusion at one end. The tolerance route escaped needing precise placement — but only for dense codes.

For sparse ones you have to actually hit those few cells. Placement precision comes straight back.

------------------------------------------------------------

Last caveat: dimensionality isn't a fixed label on a region. It's a function of area × task × recording scale, and measured low dimensionality partly reflects simple lab tasks.

Many BCIs work by deliberately keeping the task low-dimensional.

------------------------------------------------------------

So "is this system precise enough" was never a one-time question. It depends on which signal you want, where you put the electrode, how long you need it — and whether that signal is spread across cortex or lives in a handful of cells.
